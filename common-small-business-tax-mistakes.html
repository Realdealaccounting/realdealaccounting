7 Common Tax Mistakes Small Business Owners Make

Most tax problems start with ordinary habits. So do the fixes.

On this page

  4. Misclassifying workers

  6. Skipping or underpaying estimated taxes

Most tax problems in small businesses don't come from complicated schemes. They come from ordinary habits: a mixed-up bank account, a missed deadline, a receipt that never got saved. The good news is that ordinary habits are fixable.

Here are seven mistakes we see business owners make again and again, why they matter and what to do instead.

1. Mixing personal and business finances

Paying for groceries with the business card, or paying a business bill from your personal checking account, seems harmless in the moment. At tax time it turns into hours of detective work, and it makes it harder to prove which expenses were truly business expenses if the IRS ever asks.

For owners of LLCs and corporations, mixing funds can also weaken the legal separation between you and the business. The fix is simple: open a dedicated business checking account and business credit card, and use them only for business. If you need to move money to yourself, record it as an owner's draw, distribution or payroll, depending on how your business is set up.

2. Keeping incomplete or inaccurate records

Estimating expenses at year-end, rounding numbers or categorizing everything as "miscellaneous" is a recipe for errors. Inaccurate expense reporting can mean overpaying, because real deductions go uncounted, or underpaying, because personal costs slip in as business ones. Either way, the return no longer reflects what really happened.

Record transactions as they happen, attach receipts and note the business purpose for meals, travel and other expenses that may be questioned later. A monthly bookkeeping routine catches mistakes while they are still easy to fix. Our article on why proper bookkeeping matters explains what that routine looks like.

3. Missing deductions you're entitled to

Many owners leave money on the table simply because no one told them an expense counted, or because they didn't keep the paperwork. Commonly overlooked items include:

  • Business mileage, which needs a log showing dates, destinations and purpose.
  • A home office, if part of your home is used regularly and exclusively for business.
  • Startup costs incurred before the business opened, which may be partly deductible.
  • Health insurance premiums, which some self-employed people can deduct.
  • Retirement plan contributions for yourself and your employees.
  • Software, bank fees and professional fees that get buried in personal accounts.

Each of these has its own rules and limits, so it is worth confirming eligibility with your tax professional.

4. Misclassifying workers

Calling someone an independent contractor doesn't make them one. The IRS looks at the whole relationship, generally grouped into behavioral control, financial control and the type of relationship. If you control how, when and where the work is done, the person may legally be an employee.

Misclassification can lead to back payroll taxes, penalties and interest. If you're unsure, review the arrangement with a professional before the first payment, not after. Our small business payroll guide covers the basics.

Most tax penalties are not about how much you owe. They are about when and how you told the IRS.

5. Filing late and missing deadlines

Business tax deadlines depend on how the business is organized. For calendar-year businesses, partnership and S corporation returns are generally due March 15, while individual returns, including sole proprietors who file Schedule C, and C corporation returns are generally due April 15. Forms W-2 and many Forms 1099 are due by January 31.

An extension gives you more time to file, not more time to pay. Interest and penalties can still apply to tax that isn't paid by the original due date. And if you can't pay in full, file on time anyway. The penalty for failing to file is generally much steeper than the penalty for failing to pay, and the IRS offers payment options for balances you can't cover right away.

6. Skipping or underpaying estimated taxes

Business owners usually don't have taxes withheld from their income, so the IRS expects quarterly estimated payments. Owners who skip them often face two problems in April: a large balance due and an underpayment penalty on top of it.

Base your estimates on current numbers, not a guess, and revisit them when your income changes. Setting aside a percentage of every deposit in a separate account makes the payments much easier to manage. Our article on tax planning strategies explains the safe harbor rules that can help you avoid the penalty.

7. Not reporting all of your income

The IRS receives copies of many information returns, such as Forms 1099-NEC from clients and Forms 1099-K from card processors and payment apps, and it compares them with what you report. Income that shows up on those forms but not on your return can trigger a notice.

All business income is taxable whether or not you receive a form for it, including cash payments. Reconcile your deposits to your reported income before you file, so the numbers tell the same story.

How to avoid these mistakes

Nearly every mistake on this list has the same cure: steady habits and a second set of eyes. A few practical steps:

  • Keep separate business bank and credit card accounts.
  • Reconcile your books every month, not once a year.
  • Put every tax deadline on your calendar, with reminders a few weeks ahead.
  • Review estimated payments each quarter.
  • Ask before you classify a new worker or make a large purchase.
  • Start gathering documents early. Our tax season preparation checklist can help.

None of this requires you to become a tax expert. It requires a system, and someone who checks it. Our bookkeeping and tax services are built to provide exactly that.

The takeaway

Separate accounts, monthly bookkeeping and a calendar of deadlines prevent most small business tax mistakes.

This article is general information, not tax, legal or financial advice for your specific situation. Tax rules change and depend on your individual circumstances, so talk with a qualified professional before acting.
