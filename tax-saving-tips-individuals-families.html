Tax-Saving Opportunities Individuals and Families Should Understand

Credits, deductions and accounts worth knowing about before you file.

On this page

  2. Common credits for families
  3. Retirement contributions
  4. Education-related tax benefits
  5. Charitable contributions and recordkeeping
  6. The value of year-round tax planning

You don't need to own a business to benefit from tax planning. Families and individuals have their own set of deductions, credits and accounts that can lower their tax bill, as long as they know about them and keep the right records.

Tax rules change often, and eligibility usually depends on income, filing status and family situation. Use this as a starting point for a conversation with your tax professional, not as a final answer.

Deductions vs. credits: know the difference

A deduction reduces your taxable income. A credit reduces your tax bill directly, dollar for dollar. That makes a credit generally more valuable than a deduction of the same amount. Some credits are refundable, which means they can produce a refund even if you owe no tax. Others are nonrefundable and can only reduce your tax to zero.

Standard deduction or itemizing?

Every taxpayer can take either the standard deduction or itemize deductions, whichever is larger. Common itemized deductions include mortgage interest, state and local taxes up to a set limit, charitable gifts and medical expenses above a threshold based on your income. Because the standard deduction is fairly large, many households don't itemize. It's still worth checking each year, especially after a home purchase or a year with large medical bills.

Common credits for families

  • Child Tax Credit: for qualifying children under 17, subject to income limits.
  • Credit for Other Dependents: for dependents who don't qualify for the Child Tax Credit, such as older children or relatives you support.
  • Child and Dependent Care Credit: for a portion of the cost of care for a child under 13 or another qualifying person, so you can work or look for work.
  • Earned Income Tax Credit: a refundable credit for low- to moderate-income workers, with amounts that depend on income and family size.

Credit amounts and income limits are adjusted from time to time, so confirm the current figures for the year you're filing.

Retirement contributions

Saving for retirement can lower your taxes now or later, depending on the type of account.

Workplace plans

Contributions to a traditional 401(k) or 403(b) are generally made before income tax, which lowers your taxable income for the year. Many plans also offer a Roth option. Roth contributions don't reduce your taxes today, but qualified withdrawals in retirement are tax-free. If your employer offers a match, contributing enough to receive it is often one of the best returns available.

IRAs

Contributions to a traditional IRA may be deductible, depending on your income and whether you or your spouse are covered by a workplace plan. Roth IRA contributions aren't deductible, but qualified withdrawals are tax-free, and eligibility depends on income. Contributions for a given year can generally be made up to that year's tax filing deadline, usually in April.

Other options

Eligible low- and moderate-income savers may qualify for the Saver's Credit. If you're covered by a high-deductible health plan, a health savings account (HSA) offers a rare combination: contributions are deductible or made pre-tax, growth is tax-free, and withdrawals for qualified medical expenses are tax-free. The best time to plan your taxes is while the year is still happening.

Education-related tax benefits

  • American Opportunity Tax Credit: for qualified expenses during the first four years of college or other post-secondary education. It is partially refundable.
  • Lifetime Learning Credit: for a broader range of courses, including graduate school and classes to improve job skills. It is nonrefundable.
  • Student loan interest deduction: you may be able to deduct interest paid on qualified student loans, subject to income limits.
  • 529 plans: earnings grow tax-deferred, and withdrawals for qualified education expenses are free of federal income tax.

You generally can't claim both education credits for the same student in the same year, and income limits apply to both. Some states offer a deduction for 529 contributions. Florida doesn't have a state income tax, so there is no state deduction to claim here.

Charitable contributions and recordkeeping

Gifts to qualified charitable organizations can be deductible if you itemize. Recent legislation also created a limited deduction for cash gifts by people who take the standard deduction, starting with 2026 tax returns. Ask your tax professional how the current rules apply to you.

Keep the right records

  • Any cash gift: keep a bank record or a written receipt from the charity showing its name, the date and the amount.
  • Single gifts of $250 or more: get a written acknowledgment from the charity, which states whether you received anything in return.
  • Noncash gifts over $500: you generally need to file Form 8283 with your return.
  • Most noncash gifts over $5,000: a qualified appraisal is generally required.

If you're 70½ or older, a qualified charitable distribution sent directly from your IRA to a charity can be excluded from your income and can count toward any required minimum distribution.

The value of year-round tax planning

Most of these opportunities depend on decisions made during the year: how much you contribute, when you give, how you pay for education and how much tax is withheld from your paycheck. Waiting until you file leaves little room to change the outcome.

  • Review your withholding after major life changes, such as marriage, a new baby, a home purchase or a new job. The IRS offers a free Tax Withholding Estimator online.
  • Keep a folder, paper or digital, for tax documents as they arrive during the year.
  • Revisit retirement and HSA contributions each fall, while there's still time to adjust.
  • Talk with a tax professional before making big financial moves, not after.

The takeaway

Credits, retirement accounts, education benefits and good charitable records can all lower your taxes, if you plan ahead.

This article is general information, not tax, legal or financial advice for your specific situation. Tax rules change and depend on your individual circumstances, so talk with a qualified professional before acting.
