IRS Audits: What Business Owners Need to Know

How audits work, what to keep on file and how to respond calmly.

On this page
  1. What an IRS audit is
  2. Why a business may be selected
  3. Documentation every business should keep
  4. How to respond to an IRS notice
  5. Why professional representation can be valuable

Few envelopes make a business owner's stomach drop like one from the IRS. But an audit is not an accusation, and most of what determines how it goes is in your control long before the letter arrives.

What an IRS audit is

An IRS audit, which the IRS calls an examination, is a review of a tax return to confirm that income, deductions and credits were reported correctly. Being selected does not mean the IRS believes you did something wrong. Some audits end with no changes at all.

Types of audits

  • Correspondence audit: handled entirely by mail. The IRS asks for documents supporting specific items on your return.
  • Office audit: you, or your representative, meet with an IRS employee at an IRS office.
  • Field audit: an IRS agent visits your home, business or your representative's office, usually for broader reviews.

The IRS says it first contacts taxpayers about an audit by mail. It does not start audits by phone, email or text. If someone contacts you that way claiming to be from the IRS, be cautious and verify before sharing any information.

How far back can the IRS look?

Generally, the IRS includes returns filed within the last three years in an audit. If it finds a substantial error, it may add more years, but it usually doesn't go back more than six years. This is one reason record retention matters.

Why a business may be selected

The IRS doesn't publish exactly how it chooses returns, but it does describe a few general methods:

  • Computer screening: returns are scored by comparing them to norms for similar returns.
  • Document matching: information the IRS receives from third parties, such as Forms W-2, 1099-NEC and 1099-K, doesn't match what was reported.
  • Related examinations: a return may be selected because it is connected to another taxpayer being audited, such as a business partner or investor.

Selection can also be random. In practice, returns are more likely to raise questions when numbers look unusual for the type of business, when expenses seem large compared with income, when reported income doesn't match information returns or when the same item appears year after year without clear support. None of these guarantees an audit, and having them doesn't mean anything is wrong. They are simply the items worth documenting carefully.

The audit itself is short. The records that decide it are built every day of the year.

Documentation every business should keep

An audit is about proof. The burden is on you to support what you reported, so your records should make it easy:

  • Income: invoices, sales records, deposit records and the information returns you received.
  • Expenses: receipts, invoices, canceled checks and card statements, with the business purpose noted for meals, travel and similar costs.
  • Vehicle use: a mileage log showing dates, destinations, business purpose and miles.
  • Assets: purchase records, dates placed in service and sale records for equipment and property.
  • Payroll: payroll reports, filed payroll returns and worker classification records.
  • Financial statements and reconciliations that tie your books to your bank accounts and your tax return.

The IRS generally recommends keeping records that support a return for at least three years from filing, and longer in some cases, including at least four years for employment tax records. Our article on why proper bookkeeping matters explains how good records protect you.

Worth knowing

Not every notice is an auditMany IRS letters simply propose a change, ask for a missing form or request payment. Read it carefully before assuming the worst.

How to respond to an IRS notice

Whether the letter is an audit or a simpler notice, the same steps apply:

  • Don't ignore it. Problems that go unanswered usually get more expensive, and you may lose appeal rights.
  • Read it carefully. Note the notice or letter number, usually printed in a corner, the tax year involved and exactly what the IRS is asking for.
  • Mark the deadline. If you need more time, ask before the deadline passes.
  • Compare it with your return. If you agree with the change, follow the instructions. If you don't, gather documents that support your position.
  • Respond in writing and keep copies of everything you send and receive, along with proof of when you sent it.
  • Send only what is requested. Answer the questions asked, completely and accurately.

If the audit ends with changes you disagree with, you generally have the right to appeal within the IRS and, in many cases, in court.

Why professional representation can be valuable

You have the right to be represented in dealings with the IRS. CPAs, enrolled agents and attorneys can represent taxpayers once you authorize them, usually with Form 2848, Power of Attorney and Declaration of Representative. In many cases, your representative can handle communications and meetings so you don't have to.

An experienced representative knows what the IRS is likely to look for, helps you organize documents before they are submitted, keeps the discussion focused on the items under review and explains your options if you disagree with the result. Just as important, they keep you from accidentally making the situation harder.

The takeaway

Respond promptly, keep thorough records and don't hesitate to have a qualified professional represent you.
