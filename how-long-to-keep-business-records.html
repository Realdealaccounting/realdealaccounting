How Long Should You Keep Business Records?

What to keep, for how long, and how to stay organized without drowning in paper.

On this page

  2. The IRS's general guidelines
  3. Employment tax records
  4. Property and asset records
  5. What many businesses keep permanently
  6. Paper or digital?
  7. A simple retention system

Throw records away too soon and you may not be able to support your tax return if questions come up. Keep everything forever and you drown in paper. The right answer sits in between, and the IRS gives clear guidelines for finding it.

Why record retention matters

Your records are the proof behind every number on your tax return. If the IRS asks about an expense, a deduction or a source of income, you need documents that support what you reported. Good records also help you prepare accurate returns, track how your business is doing and answer questions from lenders, insurers or a future buyer.

How long you need to keep a record mostly depends on how long the IRS can review the return it supports. That window is called the period of limitations.

The IRS's general guidelines

The IRS recommends keeping records that support items on a return until the period of limitations for that return runs out. In practice, that means:

  • 3 years from the date you filed the return, in most cases. A return filed before its due date is treated as filed on the due date.
  • 3 years from filing, or 2 years from the date you paid the tax if that is later, when you file a claim for a credit or refund.
  • 6 years if you failed to report income that should have been reported and it is more than 25% of the gross income shown on the return.
  • 7 years if you claim a loss from worthless securities or a deduction for bad debt.
  • Indefinitely if you don't file a return or file a fraudulent return.

Because the three-year rule covers most situations but not all of them, many businesses simply keep tax records for seven years to be safe.

An example

Suppose a calendar-year business files its return on March 1, before the April 15 due date. Because an early return is treated as filed on the due date, the general three-year window runs from April 15 of that year, not March 1. The receipts, invoices and bank statements behind that return should be kept at least until that window closes, and longer if one of the other rules applies.

Records aren't clutter. They're the evidence behind every number you report.

Employment tax records

If you have employees, keep employment tax records for at least 4 years after the date the tax becomes due or is paid, whichever is later. That includes payroll registers, copies of filed payroll tax returns, employees' Forms W-4, records of tax deposits and copies of Forms W-2 you issued. Property and asset records

Records for equipment, vehicles, buildings and other property follow a different clock. You need them to figure depreciation each year and to calculate your gain or loss when you sell or dispose of the asset. The IRS says to keep them until the period of limitations expires for the year in which you dispose of the property.

For example, if you buy a piece of equipment this year and sell it eight years from now, the purchase records need to be kept until at least three years after you file the return reporting the sale.

Worth knowing

Check other rules tooLenders, insurance companies and state agencies may require you to keep certain records longer than the IRS does.

What many businesses keep permanently

Some documents are worth keeping for the life of the business, even when no tax rule requires it. Many advisors recommend keeping these permanently:

  • Copies of filed tax returns.
  • Formation documents, such as articles of organization or incorporation, operating agreements, bylaws and IRS letters confirming elections like S corporation status.
  • Meeting minutes and major contracts, such as ownership agreements and leases still in effect.
  • Year-end financial statements.

Paper or digital?

You don't need to keep paper. Scanned receipts and electronic records are generally acceptable, as long as they are complete, accurate and legible, and you can produce them if asked. Digital records are easier to search and back up, and they take up no space in your office.

If you go digital, store files in a secure system with regular backups, and make sure more than one person knows how to find them.

A simple retention system

  • Organize by tax year. Keep each year's records together so the whole year can be archived or disposed of at once.
  • Capture receipts as you go. Snap a photo or scan documents when they arrive, and note the business purpose where needed.
  • Separate the long-term files. Keep asset records and permanent documents apart from yearly records so they aren't destroyed by mistake.
  • Review once a year. After your return is filed, check which years have passed their retention period.
  • Dispose of records securely. Shred paper and permanently delete digital files that contain personal or financial information.

The takeaway

Keep most tax records at least three years, payroll records four, asset records until the property is sold and the period closes, and key business documents for good.

This article is general information, not tax, legal or financial advice for your specific situation. Tax rules change and depend on your individual circumstances, so talk with a qualified professional before acting.
